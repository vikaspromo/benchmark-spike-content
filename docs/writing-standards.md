## Article Audience

### Target Reader

Write for the target reader established from the assignment and practice context under [Architecture](architecture.md#understand-the-assignment). Reader persona means the reader's relevant knowledge, concern, purpose, and priorities. Do not assume every assignment addresses a patient; professional training articles must serve the medical professionals considering that training.

For patient-facing articles, address a capable adult making a personal health or appearance decision who may lack clinical knowledge. Explain unfamiliar concepts without treating the reader as inexperienced in life. First-time treatment does not imply an unsophisticated reader.

Adapt the depth of explanation to the reader's likely knowledge and decision stage. Do not invent demographics, emotions, previous treatment experiences, or preferences. Simple language must still convey useful substance; do not reduce the article to a sequence of obvious tips.

### Reader Priorities

Prioritize information according to the reader's main question. For a treatment comparison, explain the differences that affect the choice. For a first-time treatment article, explain expected results, the treatment experience, and meaningful commitments or risks. For a provider-selection article, explain supported expertise and the practice's approach. Include other information only when it helps answer that main question.

Answer the assigned question clearly, accurately, and thoroughly for the intended reader. Give supported explanations, practical expectations, and distinctions that help the reader understand the topic or make the decision it addresses.

Produce localized, treatment-specific education with useful subtopics, natural practice integration, authoritative support, and a localized call to action.

## Article Voice

Write as an informed health and aesthetics writer explaining the assigned topic and the practice to the intended reader. Be warm, direct, and assured, with the depth appropriate to [Article Audience](#article-audience). Give the article an editorial point of view by explaining what matters and why through supported details and meaningful distinctions. Respect the reader's judgment; do not adopt the tone of a basic how-to script.

Present supported practice information as part of the explanation. Make the practice, clinician, treatment, or patient experience the subject of the sentence rather than routinely narrating what a website “says,” “lists,” or “describes.” Explain what a relevant feature means for the reader instead of reciting a catalogue of equipment, credentials, or services. Preserve attribution and uncertainty when needed under [Content Types](#content-types) and [Medical Claims](#medical-claims).

Answer the reader's question directly and explain relevant facts clearly. Use conversational language, active voice, natural transitions, and real-world examples when appropriate. Develop connected explanations rather than repetitive fact lists, mechanical transitions, or closing statements that merely announce why the preceding paragraph was useful. Express necessary qualifications in plain language beside the points they qualify. Preserve the safety and consultation limits in [Medical Claims](#medical-claims).

Create warmth through attention to the reader's concerns and specific explanations. Avoid stock enthusiasm, inflated praise, a sales-script tone, repetitive wording, keyword stuffing, generic AI phrases, unnecessary technical language, long walls of text, excessive keyword bolding, and introductions that immediately start selling. Avoid generic phrases such as "In today's fast-paced world," "When it comes to," "Unlock the secrets," "Delve into," and "Game-changing." Do not copy content from competitors.

## Article Structure

### Body Word Count

The final article in the initial workload must contain 1,150–1,250 words of substantive body content, including the introduction, paragraphs, bullets, and closing call to action. Exclude the title, headings, image captions, text within images, and document comments from the count. Quality guides coverage and writing; word count confirms the resulting article's length rather than determining section content or word allocations. Never add filler or remove necessary information to meet the range.

### Opening

Establish the reader's question and give a useful starting answer in the introduction. Choose an opening appropriate to the topic and reader rather than follow a fixed introduction formula.

### Section Focus

Use descriptive, specific, reader-focused headings that answer relevant questions. Organize content into logical H2 sections. Use a clear H1/H2/H3 hierarchy as needed; H3s and a separate FAQ block are conditional on improving organization or answering reader questions. Do not require every heading level or an FAQ block in every article.

Each section must address its heading and add distinct reader value. Every paragraph, bullet, and example must support that answer through explanation, evidence, a relevant distinction, or a necessary qualification. Move misplaced material and remove tangents and repetition. Place necessary medical qualifications and safety advice under the relevant headings.

### Readability

Keep paragraphs short; most should contain one, two or three sentences. Use bullets whenever possible to improve readability. Aim for a sixth- to ninth-grade reading level, short sentences, and easy scanning while maintaining reader usefulness.

### Links

Include two to four internal content links, with two for a 300-word article and four for an article of 600 or more words. The initial workload therefore requires four internal content links. The call-to-action link does not count toward that total. Prioritize the main service page, related service pages, and relevant educational articles. The first internal link should typically point to the primary service being promoted. Do not link to the same internal destination twice.

Provider, contact, and booking pages are also useful internal destinations where relevant. Choose links for reader usefulness rather than inserting unrelated destinations to meet the count.

Include one or two external links when appropriate. Prefer authoritative sources such as the American Academy of Dermatology, Mayo Clinic, NIH, CDC, FDA, and the American Society of Plastic Surgeons. Other authoritative sources may be used. Do not link to direct competitors or directories such as Wikipedia.

### Call to Action

End each article with a clear, customized invitation and a scheduling link and/or phone number. Direct the reader toward an appropriate consultation or booking pathway. Name relevant providers when supported and useful, under [Medical Providers](#medical-providers); do not invent procedure associations or consultation terms to make the invitation more specific.

### Images

Use images to help explain the topic or patient experience under [Content Types](#content-types). Captions must use supported details. [User Design](userdesign.md#article-content-and-writing-standards) owns inline placement, caption presentation, source-link comments, and image delivery.

### Style Conventions

Follow AP Style for spelling, grammar, punctuation, capitalization, and usage, subject to BMP's conventions below. [Article Audience](#article-audience) and [Article Voice](#article-voice) govern the article's audience, tone, depth, and explanations.

- Spell out numbers one through nine; use numerals for 10 and above.
- Use BMP's custom state abbreviations when appropriate, such as MD, VA, and DC.
- Use the percent symbol.
- Capitalize professional titles only when they appear before the person's name.
- Use date forms such as June 15, 2026, and Monday, June 15.
- Use the Oxford comma for BMP consistency.
- Follow AP compound-modifier hyphenation.
- Format phone numbers as 301-555-1234 and verify the actual number.
- Use AP headline-style capitalization for headings.
- Spell business names correctly and preserve practice terminology.

## Practice-Specific Content

### Customization

Each article must include meaningful content specific to the practice, supported by evidence. No fixed percentage is required. Do not invent information or differences between practices.

Apply this test: if the article were used for another practice offering the same services, would some content need substantive rewriting or replacement because it describes this practice's own cases, clinician insights, treatment approach, patient experience, or images? Merely needing to verify the same facts for another practice does not satisfy the test. General educational explanations may remain reusable.

Prioritize supported practice material that answers the assigned question or explains the patient experience. Use useful practice evidence rather than substitute general explanations. Omit unrelated material and do not infer facts that the evidence does not establish.

### Content Types

The following kinds of material can support practice customization; they are not mandatory features of every article. Use the information available without requiring a complete account of a case, treatment policy, or care process.

- **Clinician reasoning:** Explanations of how a clinician evaluates a concern or chooses a treatment. For example, what the clinician examines when deciding whether upper-lip movement or a lack of volume is the main concern. Attribute the reasoning to the clinician when the source supports that attribution; general treatment education does not establish a clinician's decision process.
- **Patient cases:** Published accounts of a patient's concern, treatment choice, experience, or result, with or without images. A stated treatment amount or purpose can provide a useful example even when the full case history is unavailable. Describe only what the source establishes about the patient's goal, treatment, reasoning, and outcome.
- **Treatment policies and patient experience:** Concrete details about preparation, comfort options, treatment sequence, aftercare, or appointment procedures. Examples include numbing options, observation after injections, or a policy of staging treatments. Explain how the relevant details affect the patient's experience without claiming that common procedures are unique to the practice.
- **Products and technology:** Named formulations, devices, or treatment systems relevant to the topic, including supported descriptions of their purpose or reasons for selection. Distinguish product characteristics from a clinician's stated preference, and general product information from evidence that the practice uses the product.
- **Follow-up and adjustments:** Details about when results are reviewed, what the clinician checks, how concerns are handled, or how additional treatment is arranged. Use specific published details; a general promise of ongoing care does not establish a schedule, assessment, or adjustment policy.
- **Clinician observations:** Attributed practical insights, recurring patient questions, or misunderstandings that a clinician explains. These can show how experience informs care or patient education. Website FAQs may supply useful answers without establishing a named clinician's experience.
- **Quotes, testimonials, and social posts:** Approved provider quotes, genuine Google reviews or testimonials, and posts from the practice’s Facebook or Instagram accounts can add expertise, patient experience, or treatment context. Use material only when it helps answer the article’s question. Attribute it to the speaker, reviewer, or practice, and include a direct link to the specific source passage, review, testimonial, or post. Never fabricate quotations or review language. Treat patient accounts as individual experiences, not evidence of general medical outcomes.
- **Practice images:** Relevant images of the practice's clinicians, setting, equipment, or treatment examples. Distinguish images depicting the practice or its work from stock and manufacturer images; appearing on the website alone does not establish that distinction. Use images to help explain the topic or patient experience, with captions limited to supported details. An image can contribute without supplying a complete patient case.
- **Product and technology images:** Use images only for products, formulations, devices, or treatment systems that evidence confirms the practice uses and that are relevant to the article. A general product description or comparison does not establish practice use. Match the image to the confirmed product or technology; confirmation of a brand does not establish use of every formulation or model. Use imagery to support the explanation rather than as decoration. A shared manufacturer image does not by itself establish practice customization.

### Medical Providers

Include medical providers when their confirmed roles or expertise help the reader understand the treatment or choose a consultation. When several providers are relevant, do not single out one without a reason unless the assignment names that person. The article does not need to list the whole team.

State that a provider performs a treatment only when source evidence confirms that association. Otherwise, describe the provider’s supported role or expertise without assigning the treatment to them. Do not require a list of who performs each procedure when the article can answer the reader’s question without it.

### Geography

Include city, state, and service area naturally when applicable. Include neighborhoods and surrounding markets when they help explain location or service coverage. Do not force geographic keywords or imply a physical location unsupported by practice evidence.

### Medical Claims

Support each medical claim with either a link to a reputable independent medical source or evidence from the practice’s website. Use independent medical sources for general treatment facts and the practice’s website for claims about its own services and care.

Check claims from both sources before using them. Remove guarantees, misleading promises, unsupported statistics, and claims that treatment is risk-free. If the evidence leaves a claim doubtful, omit it. Use qualifications only when they accurately reflect the evidence.

Answer general treatment questions directly. Refer the reader to a clinician for individual suitability and treatment planning. Include safety advice when relevant to the article’s question; do not add unrelated warnings or repeatedly substitute “ask your provider” for an available answer.
